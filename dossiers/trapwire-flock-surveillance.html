DOSSIER ID: FF-012 · TRACK 02 · CIVIC ACCOUNTABILITY & SURVEILLANCE · PREPARED FOR BRADFORD — JULY 26, 2026

Cross-Reference Memo: TrapWire / Abraxas / Anonymizer → Flock Safety ALPR & Police Drones

⚖ VERIFIED BY PUBLIC RECORD
⚖️ AUDIT VERDICT: STRUCTURAL CONTINUITY CONFIRMED

Public regulatory filings, leaked internal logs, and municipal audit records confirm that the structural model pioneered by post-9/11 private intelligence vendors (TrapWire/Abraxas)—opaque vendor data flows, undisclosed federal backdoors, and conflict-ridden procurement—has re-emerged at 50x scale through Silicon Valley VC-backed ALPR networks (Flock Safety) and BVLOS police drone programs. An inquiry is not an adjudication.

Executive Summary

The four core historical sources (Brookings, leaked Abraxas/TrapWire trademark filings, The Nation, and ZDNet) document a private-intelligence surveillance network built in the mid-2000s by CIA veterans. By 2012, this network was exposed via the WikiLeaks Stratfor email leak—not primarily for the surveillance technology itself, but for undisclosed financial conflicts of interest between the company (TrapWire/Abraxas) and the private intelligence firm (Stratfor) marketing it to police departments and the Pentagon.

Fourteen years later, the same basic pattern is playing out at a far larger scale with Flock Safety, the license-plate-reading camera company now valued at roughly $7.5 billion and expanding rapidly into autonomous drones. The company names are different, the capital comes from venture capital instead of direct federal contracting, but the structural flaw is identical to the one Brookings flagged in TrapWire: it isn't really about whether the surveillance technology works. It is about opaque data flows, undisclosed access arrangements, and accountability gaps between the vendor, local government, and federal agencies.

Bottom line: TrapWire didn't disappear—it is still running today as a FedRAMP-authorized federal platform. Its corporate lineage (Abraxas → Cubic → Ntrepid) also produced “persona management” software for fake online identities. Meanwhile, Flock has scaled the same underlying model—private company, ambiguous oversight, federal backdoor access—to 100,000+ cameras and, since acquiring drone company Aerodome in 2024, an aerial layer that didn't exist in the TrapWire era.

Part 1 — What the Four Sources Actually Show

TrapWire / Abraxas (Brookings + Leaked Trademark Filing)

TrapWire was video-analytics software built to spot pre-attack terrorist surveillance patterns across a network of connected sites (“PersonPrint,™ VehiclePrint™”). It was developed inside Abraxas Corporation, founded post-9/11 by Richard “Hollis” Helms, a former CIA European division chief. By 2008, Abraxas was valued around $65 million and staffed with, as The Nation put it, “a couple hundred former spooks.” Cubic Corporation acquired most of Abraxas in 2010, but not TrapWire itself, which had been spun out as a separate entity.

Brookings' framing is the critical baseline: the real scandal wasn't merely that this surveillance tool existed, it was that Fred Burton, a VP at private intelligence firm Stratfor, promoted TrapWire to Stratfor's own clients while Stratfor secretly collected an 8% referral fee on every sale—without disclosing that conflict to the clients being sold to. Intelligence analyst Matthew Aid called this “constructive fraud.” Burton also briefly held a Texas Department of Public Safety intelligence post in 2009, positioning him to help steer a $1.5 million state TrapWire contract. Stratfor's leaked emails (5 million of them, hacked and published via WikiLeaks in late 2011/2012) show the firm actively chasing Pentagon, Army, Marine Corps, and big-city police contracts for the product.

Anonymizer & The Wider Network (The Nation + ZDNet/RT Reporting)

The Nation's piece widens the lens: TrapWire was one node in a larger private-intelligence industry—by their count, roughly 1,900 private intelligence corporations and nearly 5 million Americans holding security clearances. It names ChoicePoint (personal-data brokerage), and contractors like Booz Allen, Lockheed Martin, and SAIC operating in the same ecosystem.

ZDNet's original reporting traced the corporate paper trail: Cubic's own marketing described Abraxas as “A Cubic Company” even while Cubic publicly denied any tie to TrapWire; Anonymizer, the consumer online-anonymity company founded by Lance Cottrell, ended up under the same corporate umbrella. A person named Margaret A. Lee registered TrapWire Inc. in 2009 and also sat on the board of Ntrepid, a separate contractor that built “persona management” software—tools allowing one operator to manage up to ten fake online personas—under a $2.76 million U.S. Central Command contract aimed at Arabic/Persian/Urdu-language messaging (linked to the military's Operation Earnest Voice).

Part 2 — What Happened After 2013 (“What's Changed”)

  • TrapWire did not fold. It operates today under trapwire.com with a product suite (Polaris, Gauntlet, Helios, Athena, Sentry) serving federal government, school-safety, critical-infrastructure, and private-sector clients across 2,000+ sites. In 2018 it received FedRAMP authorization—granting federal agencies clearance to deploy it as a cloud platform. A 2019 Texas Monitor report found the state was actively renewing and expanding its TrapWire contract without publishing effectiveness data.
  • Anonymizer effectively ended as a consumer product. After Abraxas → Cubic → Ntrepid absorbed it, Ntrepid transitioned Anonymizer's consumer VPN customers to a different provider (Invincibull) in October 2018 and refocused on “managed attribution” work—government-facing anonymity and persona tooling, abandoning consumer privacy.
  • Nobody was meaningfully punished. The Stratfor/TrapWire referral-fee story generated a news cycle in 2012, but there is no public record of fines, prosecutions, or updated procurement-disclosure rules tracing back to it. The pattern Brookings called “sleaze” was reported, after which contracting relationships continued uninterrupted.

Part 3 — Flock Safety: Capital Scale & The Current ALPR Network

Flock Safety has deployed 100,000+ AI license-plate-reading cameras across the U.S. as of mid-2026, partnering with roughly 4,800–5,000 law-enforcement agencies. Every scan uploads to a nationwide, cross-jurisdiction searchable database.

The scandal shape is nearly identical to TrapWire's

The core issue is not “does the camera work”—it is undisclosed/unauthorized data access:

  • Mountain View, CA: 29 of 30 cameras had unauthorized statewide lookup settings active; 600,000+ searches by 250+ agencies occurred with no data-sharing agreement in place. Audits found ICE, ATF, Air Force, and a GSA Inspector General had queried the system, and Flock had no logs for parts of the period.
  • San Francisco: 299 improper inquiries were uncovered; a class action alleges 1.6 million unauthorized federal queries occurred over seven months.
  • Washington State (UW Center for Human Rights, Oct. 2025): Documented “front door” (agencies deliberately sharing with Border Patrol), “back door” (federal searches on networks that never authorized access), and “side door” access (local officers typing “ICE” into search-reason fields on the feds' behalf) across at least 18 Washington agencies—violating the state's Keep Washington Working Act.
  • Illinois: Evanston and Oak Park cancelled contracts after discovering CBP access via an undisclosed pilot program.
  • Result: 53+ municipalities across 20 states have cancelled or rejected Flock contracts—Dayton literally covered 72 cameras with trash bags—while roughly 800 new cities signed on in 2026 alone. Flock's CEO, Garrett Langley, publicly characterized opposition as “coordinated attacks” meant to “defund the police.”

Independent Accuracy & Harm Record (ACLU)

  • About 1 in 10 plate reads is misidentified; under 1% of scans connect to an actual crime.
  • Documented misuse: Kansas police used Flock to track a local newspaper op-ed critic; a Colorado officer wrongly accused a woman of car theft off a bad match; a family was held at gunpoint over a false stolen-vehicle alert; ICE/CBP have used Flock searches to pursue immigrants without warrants.

Part 4 — Drones: The New Layer TrapWire Never Had

In October 2024, Flock acquired drone company Aerodome for over $300 million, entering “Drone as First Responder” (DFR): autonomous, beyond-visual-line-of-sight (BVLOS) drones launched from fixed pads, targeting an 86-second response time and a 4-mile radius. Elk Grove, CA got the first FAA waiver for Flock's drone program in January 2025. The FAA overhauled its approval process in April 2025 and, per EFF's July 2026 review, issued more BVLOS waivers in the following ten months than in the prior seven years combined—over 1,000 agencies now hold them. Flock is also building U.S.-manufactured, NDAA-compliant drones at a new Georgia facility, targeting 100 cities by the end of 2026. Axon is the other major vendor scaling in this space.

EFF's concerns track closely with the older TrapWire critique: drones reach “backyards, roofs, through windows” that a patrol officer normally couldn't; deployments increasingly cover low-risk calls (noise complaints, welfare checks, unhoused people) rather than just emergencies; footage feeds straight into the same ALPR/camera systems; and many programs launch with little to no advance community notice. Flock has also started marketing “flying ALPRs”—drones that read plates from the air, extending the plate-tracking dragnet into three dimensions.

The Insight Worth Taking Away

The through-line isn't a corporate conspiracy connecting TrapWire directly to Flock—there is no evidence of that, and precision is critical so the real pattern doesn't get dismissed along with a fake one. The through-line is structural: a private vendor sells a surveillance capability to local government, embeds itself with federal agencies through technical “interoperability” features that local officials rarely audit, and the resulting scandal is always about undisclosed access and money, not technical capability. TrapWire's version was a hidden referral fee. Flock's version is a hidden federal search log.

What is genuinely different this time:

  1. Scale: Flock's footprint is roughly 50x TrapWire's site count and its VC-driven valuation is roughly 100x Abraxas'.
  2. Funding Source: Growth is driven by Silicon Valley venture capital hunting a public-safety addressable market rather than intelligence contracting, changing incentives (growth-at-all-costs vs. classified margins) but not the accountability gap.
  3. Accountability Response: The 53-city cancellation wave, ACLU's active campaign, and class-action litigation mark a materially bigger public pushback than TrapWire ever faced.
  4. The Aerial Layer: Drones introduce a 3D surveillance dimension with no TrapWire-era analog, accelerated by the FAA's 2025 BVLOS regulatory loosening.
